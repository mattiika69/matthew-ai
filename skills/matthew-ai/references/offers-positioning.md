# Offers and positioning

The offer course explicitly focuses on the **front-end intro offer sold to cold traffic**, not upsells or recurring services. Apply its offer-design and cash-collection guidance in that scope; extending it to another revenue stream is an application, not an established Matthew rule.

Source: S001 · Locator: 09:05–09:31. [Course scope](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=545s).

## The Core Seven is the unit of diagnosis

Matthew names **industry, ICP, offer, unit economics, big idea, messaging and proof**. Assess how they work together. The value equation helps assess an offer but does not replace the surrounding business model. Ask which of the seven is restricting the actual decision rather than mechanically grading everything.

Source: S001 · Locator: 02:29–08:51. [Offer course](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=149s).

Industry and ICP are different. “Roofing” describes a market; a marketing leader at a multi-state roofing business has different needs and buying circumstances from a small owner-operated roofer. Name the decision-maker and situation before recommending an outcome or hook.

Source: S001 · Locator: 43:38–56:09. [Industry versus buyer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=2618s).

## Specialization should improve delivery

Matthew examines whether learning from one customer can improve the next customer's work. Reusable learning can improve delivery cost, retention, proof and economics. This is a concrete reason to specialize, not merely a slogan about “niching down.” The roofing/ecommerce financial comparison in the course is a made-up model, not measured customer results.

Source: S001 · Locator: 14:58–28:20. [Specialization](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=898s).

**Application (inference):** For an agency offering unrelated services across industries, ask which work compounds through a repeatable process and which demands starting again. Do not recommend specialization solely because the agency has stopped growing; first establish whether delivery inconsistency is its constraint.

## Evaluate the industry beyond delivery

Matthew's industry scorecard considers the **average qualified customer** in the market, rather than its most attractive exception or every business in a broad category:

| Factor | What to establish |
|---|---|
| Your client's customer LTV | How much a customer is worth to the business you serve; this affects the value it can receive from your work. |
| Ease of reach | Whether you can identify qualified buyers and reach decision-makers through viable channels, including gatekeepers. |
| Qualified market size | How many buyers remain after location, company size and other ICP requirements; compare with the business you want to build. |
| Significant pain | What happens if they do nothing: lost money, impaired operations, serious consequences, or a problem they can comfortably defer. |
| Spending power | Whether the qualified buyer can actually afford the offer, even when the problem is real. |
| Market direction | Whether the market is growing, stable or declining; Matthew allows a stable market to be acceptable. |

Source: S001 · Locator: 28:29–36:46. [Industry factors](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=1709s).

His opinion-based scorecard rates each factor out of five, for 30 in total. He also warns that a severely weak factor—such as no spending power or no meaningful problem—can undermine the opportunity even when other factors look strong. Preserve that constraint judgment rather than treating the total as automatic approval. The course's illustrative LTV figures are not measured industry averages.

Source: S001 · Locator: 28:29–29:58; 36:46–37:32. [Scorecard limitations](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=1709s).

## Evaluate value in the buyer's terms

An offer should address something this buyer already cares about, with obvious value and repeatable, profitable delivery. Matthew favors needs that are legally, functionally or competitively necessary because they reduce the persuasion burden; he explicitly allows that other offers can sell.

Source: S001 · Locator: 1:00:32–1:13:14. [Recognized need](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=3632s).
Source: S001 · Locator: 2:15:45–2:16:06. [Other offers can still sell](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=8145s).
Source: S003 · Locator: 06:03–09:22. [Applied offer review](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h1D4F8AXWS4&t=363s).

When reviewing an offer, establish:

- What does the buyer receive, and why does that matter in their current situation?
- Is the result believable given the actual proof, delivery mechanism and customer's starting point?
- How long until a meaningful first benefit, and how long until the final outcome?
- What effort, implementation, inconvenience or risk does the customer bear?
- Can delivery and payment terms support the economics?

This is a synthesis. The desired result/believability/time/effort lens is Matthew's application of Alex Hormozi's value equation, not a claim Matthew invented it.

Source: S004 · Locator: 02:10–04:46. [First-client lesson](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qhAHUM1-l1c&t=130s).

Time to first value and final outcome are different. Expose a truthful early milestone instead of asking a cold buyer to value only a distant outcome. Never invent a seven-day milestone or alter fulfillment to make a hook attractive.

Source: S001 · Locator: 1:20:28–1:24:51. [First value](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=4828s).

## Cold traffic must understand the offer immediately

An offer that sells through reputation, referrals or warm content may require clearer presentation for a stranger. The ad, page and VSL must visibly explain what is sold and for whom. A benefit hidden deep in a video cannot perform the job of an obvious benefit at the decision point.

Source: S004 · Locator: 02:23–03:10; 05:07–06:03. [Cold-traffic clarity](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=qhAHUM1-l1c&t=143s).
Source: S003 · Locator: 05:15–05:44. [Visible value](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h1D4F8AXWS4&t=315s).

## Guarantees and ranges require judgment

An enormous result range and “we keep working free” can weaken the perceived offer: the buyer still wants the outcome, not more delay. Yet Matthew also discusses strong reputation/proof removing the need for a guarantee and weighs guarantees against actual success rates and economics. Do not turn this into a universal guarantee requirement or prohibition.

Source: S001 · Locator: 1:26:00–1:32:00. [Guarantees](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=5160s).
Source: S003 · Locator: 09:49–10:21; 15:26–18:48. [Ranges and applied tradeoffs](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=h1D4F8AXWS4&t=589s).

**Application (inference):** Before proposing a stronger promise, check controllability, evidence, terms, failure frequency and cost. When those are unknown, improve clarity without adding a guarantee.

## Connect the big idea, message and proof

The big idea should connect a relevant buyer belief to the offer. Research the buyer's language through calls and reviews; do not select an appealing slogan first and invent a rationale. Matthew describes collecting common problems, ranking frequency, recording different ways buyers describe them, and checking the connection to the big idea and ICP.

Source: S001 · Locator: 2:13:11–2:48:58. [Big idea and messaging](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=7991s).

Proof changes perceived likelihood. Relevant results, demonstrated understanding, useful content, reputation and time in business can all reduce buyer uncertainty. Quantity alone does not make a claim relevant to this particular buyer or objection.

Source: S001 · Locator: 2:53:06–3:02:00. [Proof](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HS8EjMBhG8o&t=10386s).
